THE BODY
OF AN EMPIRE.

How Kim Kardashian turned shapewear into a cultural phenomenon.

ADVERTISEMENT

There is something particularly fascinating about a brand that becomes recognisable before its name is even mentioned.

The muted neutrals. The sculptural silhouettes. The almost clinical simplicity of its campaigns. You could remove the SKIMS logo from an advertisement and still recognise the world it belongs to.

That is not simply good marketing. It is the result of a carefully established identity.

When Kim Kardashian co-founded SKIMS in 2019, she already possessed something most emerging businesses spend years trying to acquire: attention. Her image was familiar, her aesthetic influential, and her relationship with popular culture almost impossible to separate from the conversations surrounding it.

But celebrity alone does not explain what SKIMS has become.

The brand entered an established market and helped transform the way shapewear was perceived. Products traditionally associated with concealment were presented through a language of confidence, desirability and contemporary fashion. What was once something to hide became something worth showing.

“SKIMS did not need to invent a new desire. It needed to change the cultural meaning of an existing one.”

And perhaps that is where its most interesting achievement lies.

Rather than relying exclusively on Kardashian's fame, SKIMS developed a visual identity that could be repeated, recognised and expanded. Its campaigns introduced different personalities, its collections entered new categories, and its cultural associations continued to evolve.

Yet the brand remained unmistakably itself.

The celebrities changed. The products expanded. The identity endured.

This is what separates a recognisable brand from a business that simply attracts attention.

Attention introduces people to your world. Identity gives them a reason to remember it.

THE TAKEAWAY / YOUR OWN BRAND

What can your brand learn from SKIMS?

Consider what makes your business recognisable beyond its name or logo.

If someone encountered your campaign without seeing your brand name, would they recognise it? Does your visual language communicate a consistent idea? More importantly, does your audience understand what makes your offering desirable beyond its immediate function?

These are not questions reserved for companies with celebrity founders or substantial marketing budgets.

An emerging fashion label, recording artist or creative business can begin by identifying one central idea it wants to own in the minds of its audience.

Then examine whether its imagery, messaging, products and public presentation consistently reinforce that idea.

“Recognition is not created by repeating the same advertisement. It is developed by repeatedly communicating the same identity.”

SKIMS offers a compelling example of this principle, but translating it into an effective strategy requires more than copying a colour palette or borrowing an aesthetic.

It requires understanding the relationship between perception, positioning and consumer desire.

And that is where the real work begins.
