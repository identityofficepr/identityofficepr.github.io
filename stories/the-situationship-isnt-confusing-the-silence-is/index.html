DATING / EMOTIONAL AMBIGUITY / 18 NOV 2026

The situationship isn't confusing. The silence is.

You do couple things, have couple arguments and feel couple feelings. The only thing missing is the conversation about what any of it means.

AFTERIIMAGE. EDITORIAL DESK · ANALYSIS & PERSPECTIVE · 18 NOVEMBER 2026

At some point you have to ask why a person can sleep beside you, know your coffee order, know your friends and send you a good-morning message every day, yet the question 'What are we?' is treated like a legally binding contract.

Situationships occupy the strange territory between freedom and fear. The arrangement can be genuinely consensual and satisfying when both people want something open-ended. It can also become a place where one person receives the benefits of a relationship while leaving the other to negotiate alone with uncertainty.

Ambiguity is not the same as freedom

Freedom requires information. People need a reasonable understanding of what they have and what they are choosing. Without that, the lack of a label may protect one person's options at the expense of another person's ability to make informed decisions.

Being uninterested in formal commitment is not inherently unkind. Promising nothing while behaving romantically is also not necessarily dishonest. Trouble begins when important expectations are avoided because saying them aloud could change the arrangement.

Someone who wants exclusivity and someone who wants to date multiple people can be perfectly decent individuals who are wrong for each other. Concealing that difference is not a superior form of romantic sophistication.

The evidence on trust is more interesting than the memes

A 2026 study published in Sexuality & Culture analysed reports from 261 emerging adults about their recent romantic experiences. Situationship status was associated with lower levels of reported trust and perceived partner trust across several communication contexts. The study cannot establish that situationships cause distrust; its cross-sectional design has important limits.

Communication frequency was generally associated with trust, although the relationships were not always linear. More messages are not an automatic solution. A hundred affectionate texts can coexist with the absence of a single necessary conversation.

That is what the 'good morning' economy gets wrong. Consistency has meaning, but the content of communication matters. A person can be highly available for flirtation and curiously unavailable when the subject is mutual expectations.

Chemistry can be excellent at postponing the truth

Physical attraction and emotional closeness are not substitutes for knowing what each person wants. In fact, strong chemistry can make the ambiguity easier to tolerate. The nights are good; the messages are sweet; the unasked question gets deferred to another Sunday.

People often say that asking for clarity will ruin the mood. Sometimes it does. But the mood is not a relationship agreement. If an arrangement collapses because one participant accurately describes their hopes, the clarity may be painful and still be useful.

Labels can be too rigid for some people, particularly when they are exploring their identity, capacity for commitment or preferred relationship structure. Clarity need not mean forcing a traditional definition. 'I want to keep this casual and nonexclusive' is clarity too.

The question underneath 'What are we?'

People may be asking several different things with the same sentence: Are you seeing anyone else? Can I rely on you? Are we moving toward commitment? What happens if one of us develops deeper feelings? Do I need to protect myself emotionally or sexually?

The better conversation may start with those concrete questions rather than a title. Different answers can be negotiated. Some cannot. That difference is better discovered through honesty than through detective work on someone's social-media following.

Being the easy-going person who never asks can feel powerful in a culture that treats needing someone as embarrassment. But pretending not to want something does not erase the wanting.

No one wins by pretending to be less invested

The point is not that every undefined connection should become official. It is that a mutually chosen ambiguity and a strategically maintained ambiguity are not the same.

A relationship can begin without a plan and find its shape over time. What matters is whether both people have permission to speak honestly as it changes. When that permission disappears, the title is not the real absence.

The problem is not always that you don't know what to call it. Sometimes the problem is that you are afraid of what the other person would say if you asked.
