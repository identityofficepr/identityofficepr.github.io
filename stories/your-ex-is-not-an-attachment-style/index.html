PSYCHOLOGY / MODERN LOVE / 18 NOV 2026

Your ex is not an attachment style.

Everyone is an avoidant, everyone is anxious, and apparently nobody is simply inconsistent. Has relationship psychology become a way to avoid the actual conversation?

AFTERIIMAGE. EDITORIAL DESK · ANALYSIS & PERSPECTIVE · 18 NOVEMBER 2026

Somewhere between the breakup podcast, the TikTok carousel and the 1 a.m. search history, romantic disappointment acquired a remarkably technical vocabulary. A person doesn't reply? Avoidant. You want reassurance? Anxious. You stop caring? Secure. The language can sound deeply self-aware even when it has become another way of describing someone you barely know.

Attachment theory is real. Its takeover of ordinary dating conversation is something else entirely. The problem isn't that people are learning about psychology; it's that a framework intended to explain patterns in close relationships is being used as a personality quiz for strangers.

A useful theory becomes a universal diagnosis

Adult attachment research often examines two broad dimensions: anxiety about rejection and discomfort with closeness or reliance on others. These orientations can influence how people interpret conflict, reassurance, intimacy and separation. The concepts offer a vocabulary for patterns, not an instant diagnosis or a permanent character verdict.

A 2019 review of adult attachment emphasises emerging debates and the potential for change. Earlier experiences matter, but they do not completely determine adult relationships. Security can vary over time and across relationships. That complexity gets lost when a comment section confidently assigns somebody a category based on a screenshot of three messages.

Someone might withdraw because intimacy feels frightening. They might also be exhausted, busy, undecided, uninterested or trying to set a reasonable boundary. The behaviour matters; a stranger's invented explanation for it is not evidence.

The comforting fantasy of having a label

Labels soothe ambiguity. Saying someone is avoidant can sound more manageable than accepting that they do not want the relationship you want. It produces a story in which the problem has an explanation, and an explanation can feel like hope.

The same temptation exists in reverse. Someone can use 'I'm anxious' to turn repeated monitoring or demands for constant reassurance into an immutable identity. Understanding vulnerability does not excuse behaviour that harms another person. And having avoidant tendencies is not a moral failure or proof that someone cannot love.

Psychological language is most useful when it improves accountability: 'When communication disappears without explanation, I feel uncertain' is a statement a partner can respond to. 'You are a dismissive avoidant and this is your childhood trauma talking' is an interpretation that may shut the conversation down.

The difference between intimacy and access

Digital life encourages the assumption that being reachable means being available. We can see someone online, so we begin to treat a response as a measurement of affection. But access to a phone is not the same as capacity for a difficult conversation, just as needing space is not the same as abandoning a relationship.

Patterns matter more than individual timestamps. Does the person return to the conversation? Can the two of you discuss needs without punishment? Is the relationship generally reciprocal? Those are more revealing questions than whether their reply arrived after twelve minutes or twelve hours.

Attachment anxiety and avoidance can make stressful couple interactions harder, according to a review in Nature Reviews Psychology. That same research discusses how supportive partner responses may help buffer insecurity. In other words, the focus can be on the interaction, not merely the presumed defect of one participant.

An explanation is not an obligation

You can understand why somebody struggles with closeness and still decide the relationship is not meeting your needs. Compassion doesn't create a contract to endure indefinite uncertainty. Equally, receiving an anxious message does not give someone permission to mock or punish a partner for caring.

The most useful question is not 'what attachment style are they?' but 'what happens when we tell each other what we need?' You cannot read the inside of a stranger's nervous system from a social post. You can notice whether their actions align with their words.

Psychology should make love less mysterious in ways that help people communicate. It should not become a sophisticated method of avoiding a simple, difficult answer.
