Looking expensive is not the same as having style.

Quiet luxury gave us a uniform. Personal taste was supposed to be the point.

AFTERIIMAGE. EDITORIAL DESK · ORIGINAL COMMENTARY · 18 NOVEMBER 2026

The price tag has become an aesthetic

For a while, dressing well was sold as a problem that could be solved by looking wealthy. Neutral knitwear, polished hair, a structured bag and no visible logos: an entire visual vocabulary emerged around appearing expensive. It was neat, reproducible and extremely convenient for people selling products. It also flattened the difference between taste and status.

Luxury is not a colour palette

An expensive garment can be beautifully constructed, but beige does not become luxurious by association. A cheap outfit can have extraordinary proportion, wit and personal significance. Real quality involves fabric, fit, finish, durability and the thought behind the design. None of those properties can be reliably inferred from a muted photograph.

The uniform disguised itself as individuality

Once an aesthetic becomes a set of instructions, everyone can perform it. The same silhouettes circulate through lookbooks, shopping edits and short videos until an outfit begins to look less like a decision and more like compliance. This does not make minimalism bad. It makes the idea that minimalism is the only sophisticated taste remarkably narrow.

Style has memory

The most compelling wardrobes are not always the most coordinated. They carry traces of the person wearing them: a jacket inherited from a parent, a vintage piece found by accident, a silhouette repeated for years, a colour that should not work but somehow does. Those choices resist the logic of a single trend because they are connected to experience.

Taste requires the possibility of getting it wrong

A genuinely personal wardrobe involves risk. Sometimes the proportions are strange. Sometimes a colour combination is divisive. Sometimes the outfit is more expressive than flattering. The obsession with looking expensive discourages that experimentation because the goal is external approval. Taste becomes interesting precisely when it is not universally agreed upon.

The better question

Instead of asking whether an outfit looks rich, ask what it communicates and whether the wearer meant to say it. Does it move well? Does it belong to the person's life? Does it feel considered rather than copied? Style is not a financial statement. It is a way of making choices visible, and the best choices are not always the safest ones.
