Is it cheating if it's only in the DMs?

A like is not an affair. A private conversation is not necessarily betrayal. But secrecy changes the temperature of almost everything.

AFTERIIMAGE. EDITORIAL DESK · ANALYSIS & PERSPECTIVE · 18 NOVEMBER 2026

The couple is at dinner. One phone lights up. Nothing has technically happened. No hotel room, no obvious declaration, no cinematic betrayal. Just a conversation that would suddenly become very difficult to explain if the screen were turned around.

Modern relationship discourse has a name for nearly every ambiguous interaction: micro-cheating, orbiting, breadcrumbing, soft flirting. The terminology is louder than the evidence in most individual cases. Yet the underlying question is painfully ordinary: when does attention given elsewhere become something you have taken away from the person beside you?

The internet wants a universal rulebook

Some couples consider flirtation a breach of exclusivity. Others have explicitly agreed that flirting is harmless. Some are nonmonogamous, with boundaries that bear little resemblance to conventional assumptions. An emoji, an ex's photo or a private joke cannot be interpreted without understanding those agreements.

The American Psychological Association's 2026 discussion of infidelity notes that definitions vary, especially around emotional and digital behaviour. What matters is not simply whether strangers approve of an act, but what the people involved have agreed and what has been concealed.

That does not mean anything can be justified by claiming different boundaries after the fact. Agreements need to be communicated in advance. A relationship cannot operate fairly when one person quietly rewrites the rules in private.

Why secrecy feels different from privacy

A 2024 study in Personal Relationships asked 135 people to judge scenarios involving direct messages outside a romantic relationship. Participants treated secrecy as the strongest contextual factor in whether an otherwise mundane interaction seemed unfaithful; frequency came next. That is a study of perceptions, not a test that can determine whether your partner is cheating.

Still, the distinction between privacy and secrecy deserves attention. Privacy protects a person's legitimate inner life and independent friendships. Secrecy can involve actively hiding behaviour because it conflicts with a shared understanding. The two are not interchangeable.

A right to privacy does not disappear inside a relationship. Neither does a partner's right to ask whether an agreed boundary has been broken. The goal is not device surveillance; it is enough honesty that surveillance doesn't become the assumed price of intimacy.

The emotional affair is not always romantic at the beginning

An emotional connection outside a partnership can be entirely healthy. Friends, colleagues and family should not be treated as threats to a couple's exclusivity. The relevant issue is whether a connection has moved into concealed romantic territory or is being used to sustain a parallel relationship inconsistent with the couple's commitments.

The line may become clearer when someone begins deleting chats, misrepresenting the nature of the connection or investing in flirtation while pretending the relationship is purely practical. These patterns do not prove infidelity, but they warrant a conversation about trust.

Romantic attention becomes messy when the public story and the private story diverge. One person thinks they're simply being friendly. The other thinks they're keeping an option warm. The couple at home may not even know a second story exists.

When 'you are overreacting' is the real problem

A partner who asks about a boundary may be insecure, mistaken or justified. That is something two people can explore. Dismissing every question as crazy can prevent necessary accountability; treating every outside interaction as suspicious can become controlling.

The most productive conversation is specific. What behaviour upset you? What had you agreed? Was something intentionally hidden? What would rebuild trust without making either person surrender their independence?

If someone needs to use interrogation, humiliation or constant tracking to feel secure, the relationship has a separate safety problem. No definition of faithfulness makes coercive control acceptable.

The verdict nobody online can deliver

Is liking a story cheating? No universal answer. Is maintaining a secret, sexually charged conversation inconsistent with an agreement about exclusivity? It may well be. The distinction cannot be made by an anonymous poll.

The point of discussing digital infidelity is not to expand the list of punishable behaviours until ordinary friendship becomes impossible. It is to make expectations explicit before a private screen turns into a public humiliation.

Ultimately, the most revealing question is not 'Could I get away with this?' It is 'Could I describe this honestly to the person I chose to be with?'
